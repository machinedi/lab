This is the longer story behind the other posts here. I work an ordinary high-tech job at a software startup that has been going through a hard time with money.

When that happens, the work doesn't shrink. It spreads. Fewer people hold more threads, and a lot of them ended up with me.

The order of things mattered more than any single tool, so I'll tell it in order.

### Too many threads

On a typical day I'd find the root cause of a fault and write the fix spec for the product team, so they wouldn't have to rediscover it. I'd coordinate hardware parts between suppliers, which is mostly following who promised what and when. I'd run trainings and demos, and write or update customer documentation.

None of it is hard on its own. The problem was the number of tasks and how often I switched between them.

At the end of the day I'd done a lot and thought very little. There was no room left to ask whether the way we worked could be better.

### Writing the day down

Part of the push came from home. I'd been using personal agents there for a long time: Hermes, OpenClaw, and the assistant I chat with at home every day. At some point I realised I wanted that kind of help at work too.

So in the spring I did something unglamorous. I wrote down what I actually do every day, step by step, the way you'd explain it to someone covering for you.

Then I asked of each step: does this need me, or only someone who remembers to do it?

In May and June most of my work with Claude looked like this. Short prompts, small analyses, a lot of correcting. I was learning what to ask for.

### The mail came first

Email was eating the best part of my day, so it went first.

Three inboxes, roughly 80 to 100 emails a day by my own count. About 30 of them were relevant and needed me to read or reply.

Mixed in were fault reports from important customers who don't always use the ticket systems. They just write an email. Missing one mattered, and so did knowing about it in real time.

And it was happening in the morning, when I'm sharpest.

Looking closely, the mail split into two kinds. Some messages need a reply now. Others ask for a specific piece of work, often the same each time, which means it can be written down as a process.

So sorting became a routine: read, decide which kind it is, then either flag it for me or start the matching process.

### One thing led to another

Once mail worked, I started seeing other work the same way.

A routine went over the ticket systems and pushed cases that had stopped moving. Another followed the user surveys on the platform, and linked technical complaints to a live product analytics dashboard showing what happened to that customer at the time.

One example. A fault report came in through the app, with negative feedback around it. From the feedback I could see that sharing certain files through one specific path was failing. The analytics showed the same pattern for those users. A vague complaint became a clear fault I could describe to the product team.

Each loop I closed gave me a little time back. I spent it looking for the next thing that still depended on me remembering it.

Sorting mail also showed a gap. Some emails were configuration questions, and answering them needed something that knows how each setup is configured and where its knowledge ends. That became its own project in July and August, which I write about in the Knowledge section.

Meanwhile the routines needed a home. They started as a few small dashboards, one per process. Over the summer those grew into one web app that collected what all the agents found, drafted and waited on.

### The cost

This is the part I'd skip if I were selling something.

Some days the automation was heavier than it needed to be. A routine did many things in a row, and I couldn't see in real time what it had actually done.

The work was faster. I couldn't say it was better. And without that, I couldn't trust it, so I'd check, and then I was doing the work and checking the thing that was supposed to do it.

Those questions changed what I was looking for. Less "how much can I automate", more "what's the leanest way to run this routine, so I can see what it did and why".

### Only decisions come back

That question led to my work agent, Tony. In late September I started it in a separate repository, so I could learn without breaking the tools I relied on every day. By early October it had replaced the web app.

Tony reads my email, messages and meetings, builds its own tasks from them, and moves them forward within the permissions I set. Meetings come in through a small note taker I built. It runs only when everyone in the meeting agrees, and it transcribes on my own machine.

Whatever needs judgment comes back to me as a decision: accept, change, postpone or refuse. It doesn't send anything in my name.

The first versions had the old problem in a new form. In mid-October I counted the previous sixteen days: around fourteen proposals a day, and 26 approvals of mine that never actually ran.

I stopped, went through it question by question, and cut it back. Now everything I approve is checked against what really got done.

How the decisions, permission rules and memory fit together is in [Building a work partner, one loop at a time](/agent-building/building-a-work-partner/).

Today my work agent does most of my computer work, or at least that's how it feels to me. What's left is the part that needs me.

### Attention back

The first change was small. I stopped checking whether something had fallen through the cracks. That habit used to run in the background all day.

With the time that came back, I started booking focus time with myself. Deeper work, and research into things that were already built, just to see if they could be better. Some were fine as they were. Some weren't.

A few colleagues asked how I work, so I sat with them for short sessions on their own methods. Explaining it showed me which parts of my setup are about me, and which might work for someone else.

### What I'm still unsure about

I started this partly because of the agents I use at home, so that's also how I measure it.

Some parts work very well. The agents' work is managed in one place, tasks are arranged the way I think about them, I can see what each one is doing, and memory is handled carefully, with me confirming what it keeps.

The conversation itself is where I'm not there yet. Talking to Tony still doesn't feel like talking to the assistant I chat with at home. At home I can think out loud and it follows me. At work it feels more like operating a tool.

I don't fully know why. It may be the voice and memory it brings into a chat. It may be that I gave my work agent so many rules that there's little room left for it to just talk. That's what I'm working on now.

I'm also still the bottleneck. Every decision passes through me, and on busy days I'm asked too often. I haven't found the right line between asking me and acting alone, and I'm not sure how much of this carries over to a different job with different tools.
